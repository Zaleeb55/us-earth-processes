About the United States

The United States stretches across the middle of North America, from the Atlantic Ocean to the Pacific Ocean, and includes Alaska and Hawaii. It has 50 states, its capital is Washington, D.C., and more than 340 million people live there.

History and culture: The country declared its independence from Great Britain in 1776 and adopted its Constitution in 1787. Because people have moved here from all over the world, American culture is a mix of many traditions, foods, languages, and religions.

Economy: The United States has the largest economy in the world. Its wide range of landscapes supports farming in the Midwest, energy production in Texas and the West, technology in California, and tourism at national parks like the Grand Canyon and Yellowstone.

I was born and raised in Prescott, Arizona, a few hours' drive from the Grand Canyon, and I now live in South Jordan, Utah, with my wife and son. For work I install fire sprinkler systems on job sites from Farmington to St. George, so I see a lot of Utah's mountains, valleys, and red rock country up close. One Interesting Fact About the Physical Geography of the United States

The Grand Canyon in Arizona is about 277 river miles long and up to 6,000 feet deep. The rocks at the bottom are close to 2 billion years old, but the Colorado River carved the canyon itself in only the past five or six million years.

Four Maps of the United States

Map 1 · Ancient map

The Waldseemüller World Map (1507)

This German world map by Martin Waldseemüller is the first known map to use the name "America" for the New World. The Library of Congress calls it "America's birth certificate," and it is on permanent display in the Thomas Jefferson Building in Washington, D.C. (Library of Congress, 2001).

Map 2 · Early national map

Abel Buell's Map of the United States (1784)

Printed in 1784, just months after the Treaty of Paris ended the Revolutionary War, this was the first map of the new United States drawn and printed in America by an American. It shows the young country stretching from the Atlantic coast to the Mississippi River ("Even in 1784 America," 2014).

Map 3 · Boundary changes over time

Territorial Acquisitions of the United States

This map shows how the country's boundaries grew over time, from the original territory of 1783 through the Louisiana Purchase (1803), the annexation of Texas (1845), the Oregon Territory (1846), the Mexican Cession (1848), the Gadsden Purchase (1853), Alaska (1867) and Hawaii (1898) (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.).

Map 4 · Modern map

Modern Map, Albers Conic Equal-Area Projection

This modern U.S. Geological Survey map uses the Albers conic equal-area projection, which keeps the sizes of states accurate and is commonly used for maps of the United States. It also shows the country's position on the North American continent (Alpha, 1983).

Questions

1. On which continent and in what general area is your country located?

The United States is located on the continent of North America. The 48 contiguous states lie in the middle of the continent, between Canada to the north and Mexico to the south, with the Atlantic Ocean to the east and the Pacific Ocean to the west. Alaska sits in the far northwest of the continent, bordering Canada, and Hawaii is an island chain in the central Pacific Ocean.

2. What is the latitudinal range of your country?

The 48 contiguous states range from about 24°32' N at Key West, Florida, to about 49°23' N at the Lake of the Woods in Minnesota. Including all 50 states, the range runs from 18°55' N at Ka Lae, Hawaii, to 71°23' N at Point Barrow, Alaska (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-a).

3. During what time periods or years has your country existed?

The United States declared its independence from Great Britain on July 4, 1776. Great Britain recognized its independence in the Treaty of Paris in 1783, and the current federal government began under the Constitution in 1789. The country has existed continuously from 1776 to the present, growing from 13 states to 50.

4. Are there any famous cartographers from your country or cartographers from other countries who influenced map making in your country?

Martin Waldseemüller (German, about 1470–1520): Waldseemüller was a German cartographer whose 1507 world map was the first to label the New World "America." He named it after the explorer Amerigo Vespucci, mistakenly believing Vespucci, rather than Christopher Columbus, had discovered it. His map gave the continent, and eventually the country, its name. The Library of Congress acquired the only known surviving copy, and it is now on permanent display in Washington, D.C. (Library of Congress, 2001). See Map 1 above.

Abel Buell (American engraver): Buell was a Connecticut engraver, inventor and metalsmith. As a young man he was convicted of counterfeiting and punished by having part of his ear cut off and his forehead branded. His biggest cartographic accomplishment was his 1784 map, the first map of the United States made and printed in America by an American, completed just six months after the Treaty of Paris. Only seven original copies are known to survive, and the best-preserved one is held by the Library of Congress ("Even in 1784 America," 2014). See Map 2 above.

Week 3

Plate Tectonics, Earthquakes, and Volcanoes

The United States sits almost entirely on the North American Plate, but its western edge, Alaska, and Hawaii are some of the most geologically active places on Earth. Plate movement built the country's mountains, gave it valuable rocks and minerals, and still causes the earthquakes and eruptions people prepare for today, including along the Wasatch Fault a few miles from my home in South Jordan.

Topic 1

Tectonic Plates and Natural Disasters

Is the United States near plate boundaries?

Yes, mostly in the West. The East Coast is in the middle of the North American Plate, far from any boundary, which is why it has few big earthquakes or volcanoes. The active boundaries are:

  • Transform boundary (California): The San Andreas Fault is where a thin strip of western California, riding on the Pacific Plate, slides north-northwest past the rest of North America. The boundary is really a zone of faults about 60 miles wide (National Park Service, n.d.-c).
  • Convergent boundary (Pacific Northwest): Along the Cascadia Subduction Zone, an oceanic plate sinks beneath the North American Plate from northern California to British Columbia. As the plate sinks it heats up and releases fluids and melt, which feed the Cascade volcanoes ("Scientists unravel," n.d.).
  • Convergent boundary (Alaska): In southern Alaska the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the North American Plate, the source of the 1964 Great Alaska Earthquake (U.S. Geological Survey, 2014).
  • Hotspots, not boundaries: Hawaii and Yellowstone sit over hotspots inside plates. Hawaii is in the middle of the Pacific Plate, which is why its volcanoes are far from any boundary.

Does the U.S. have earthquakes, volcanoes, and tsunamis?

Yes, all three. A few major examples:

  • Tsunami, 1700: A magnitude ~9 Cascadia earthquake sent a tsunami across the Pacific that damaged villages in Japan (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-c).
  • Earthquake and tsunami, 1964: The magnitude 9.2 Alaska earthquake killed 128 people, 113 of them from the tsunami (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-d).
  • Volcano, 1980: Mount St. Helens killed 57 people (U.S. Geological Survey, 2005).
  • Volcano, 2018: Kīlauea's lava destroyed 700 homes in Hawaii (National Park Service, n.d.-a).

What rocks and minerals are found here, and how do they help the economy?

The United States mines a huge variety of resources. The most valuable nonfuel product is not gold but crushed stone, the rock used in roads, concrete, and buildings. It was worth $26.8 billion in 2025, about 24% of all nonfuel mineral value (American Ceramic Society, 2026). Nevada was the top mineral-producing state for the second year in a row, mostly from gold, and Arizona passed Texas for second place thanks to copper, molybdenum, gold, and silver (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026). Wyoming's Powder River Basin produces the most coal of any state (U.S. Energy Information Administration, n.d.).

Utah is a big part of this story. The Bingham Canyon Mine in the Oquirrh Mountains, just west of where I live, is the largest open-pit copper mine in the world, about 2.5 miles across and half a mile deep (Notarianni, n.d.). Its operator, Rio Tinto Kennecott, expects to produce close to one million tonnes of refined copper from 2026 to 2032 (Rio Tinto Kennecott, n.d.).

How do minerals affect jobs and exports? Where are they exported to?

Mining supports jobs directly at mines, smelters, and refineries, and indirectly in the construction, manufacturing, and technology industries that use the materials. In 2023 the United States exported $361 billion in mineral products (a category that includes fuels as well as ores and stone). The top buyers were Mexico, Canada, China, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom (Observatory of Economic Complexity, n.d.).

Are there other impacts?

Economic: The U.S. still depends on imports for many minerals. It relied on China as a major import source for 14 of the 33 critical minerals it imports most, and China restricts exports of 14 minerals to the U.S. (U.S. Geological Survey, 2026). That makes supply chains for things like batteries and electronics a national security issue.

Environmental: Mining can pollute water. Historic mining in the Oquirrh Mountains created two groundwater plumes under southwest Salt Lake County with sulfate levels above Utah's drinking water standards, and the state has worked since 1986 to clean them up (Utah Department of Environmental Quality, n.d.). Those plumes sit under cities like South Jordan, so this impact is close to home for me.

Close to home

I remember the Magna earthquake. It hit at 7:09 a.m. on March 18, 2020, and was the largest earthquake along the Wasatch Front since pioneers arrived in 1847 (Utah Geological Survey, n.d.). It was a reminder that the "Big One" on the Wasatch Fault is a real risk for families like mine.

Does the U.S. experience significant earthquakes?

Yes. Earthquakes are most common in California, the Pacific Northwest, Alaska, Hawaii, and along the Wasatch Front in Utah. Historical examples:

  • 1700 Cascadia earthquake: A magnitude ~9 quake on the Cascadia Subduction Zone. There were no written records in America yet, but the tsunami it created crossed the Pacific and damaged coastal villages in Japan, which is how scientists dated it (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-c).
  • 1906 San Francisco earthquake: A magnitude 7.8 rupture along nearly 300 miles of the San Andreas Fault. Shaking and fire destroyed almost 5 square miles of the city, killed at least 3,000 people, and left 225,000 homeless (U.S. Geological Survey, 2006).
  • 1964 Great Alaska Earthquake: At magnitude 9.2, it is the largest recorded earthquake in U.S. history and the second-largest ever recorded worldwide (U.S. Geological Survey, 2014). It and its tsunami took 128 lives (U.S. Geological Survey, n.d.-d).
  • 2020 Magna, Utah earthquake: A magnitude 5.7 quake near Salt Lake City (Utah Geological Survey, n.d.).

What is the government doing to help citizens prepare?

  • National program: Congress created the National Earthquake Hazards Reduction Program (NEHRP) in 1977. Under it, FEMA develops risk-reduction tools and supports earthquake-resistant building codes (Federal Emergency Management Agency, 2021).
  • Early warning: The USGS ShakeAlert system can send phone alerts seconds before shaking arrives. It now covers more than 50 million people in California, Oregon, and Washington, and it can also automatically slow trains and open firehouse doors (U.S. Department of the Interior, 2021).
  • Drills: More than 19 million people practiced "Drop, Cover, and Hold On" during the 2024 Great ShakeOut drill. Utah holds its own Great Utah ShakeOut (Federal Emergency Management Agency, 2024).

Topic 4

Mountain Ranges

Which major mountain ranges are in the U.S.?

  • Rocky Mountains: Stretch more than 3,000 miles from Canada to New Mexico and form the Continental Divide, the line that decides whether water flows to the Atlantic or the Pacific. The highest peak is Mount Elbert in Colorado at 14,440 feet ("Rocky Mountains," n.d.). The Wasatch Range above Salt Lake City is part of the Rockies' western edge and was lifted along the Wasatch Fault.
  • Cascade Range: An 800-mile chain of volcanoes from British Columbia to northern California, built by subduction ("Scientists unravel," n.d.). These are young, steep, snow-covered stratovolcanoes. The tallest is Mount Rainier at 14,410 feet (4,392 m) ("Climbing the occasionally cataclysmic Cascades," n.d.).
  • Appalachian Mountains: Much older, rounded mountains in the East. They were lifted when continents collided to form the supercontinent Pangea around 320 to 290 million years ago and once may have been as tall as the Himalayas ("The mountain range that crosses the most states," n.d.). Erosion has worn them down, and the highest peak today is Mount Mitchell in North Carolina at 6,684 feet (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.).

How do they affect weather, water, and culture?

Weather: Mountains force moist air to rise, cool, and drop rain or snow on the windward side, leaving a dry "rain shadow" behind them. That is why western Washington is rainy while the land east of the Cascades is dry, and why much of Utah and Nevada is desert.

Water: Mountain snowpack works like a giant reservoir. The Colorado River supplies water to about 40 million people, and 60% to 85% of its water starts as snowpack in Colorado, Utah, and Wyoming (Colorado Sun, n.d.). In Utah, the snowpack in the Wasatch is what fills our reservoirs every spring.

Culture: Mountains shape how people live and play: ski resorts and national parks in the Rockies, hiking on the Appalachian Trail, and mining towns throughout the West.

Did they provide protection or affect trade routes?

Historically, the Appalachians were a natural barrier that kept early American settlement close to the Atlantic coast. The Cumberland Gap gave settlers a way west, and it was so important that armies fought over it in the Civil War (Encyclopaedia Britannica, n.d.). Today, highways and rail lines follow mountain passes, and goods still have to be moved over or around the ranges, but tunnels and interstates have made them much less of a barrier than they once were.

Topic 5

Volcanoes

Does the U.S. have significant volcanoes?

Yes. Most are in Alaska, Hawaii, and the Cascades, plus Yellowstone in Wyoming. Some of the most important:

How do volcanoes impact the region?

  • Fertile soil: Volcanic ash and weathered lava break down into rich soil, which is part of why Hawaii and the Pacific Northwest grow so many crops and forests.
  • Tourism: Hawaiʻi Volcanoes, Yellowstone, and Mount St. Helens National Volcanic Monument draw visitors to see lava, geysers, and blast zones.
  • New land: Kīlauea's 2018 eruption added 875 acres of new land to the Big Island (National Park Service, n.d.-a).
  • Threats: Lava flows, ash, poisonous gases, and lahars (volcanic mudflows) threaten nearby towns, roads, and airplanes.

Historical eruptions and their effects

Mount St. Helens, 1980: On May 18, 1980, a magnitude 5.1 earthquake caused the north side of the volcano to collapse in the largest landslide ever witnessed. The landslide and sideways blast destroyed 230 square miles of forest and killed 57 people, including USGS geologist David Johnston (U.S. Geological Survey, 2005). The summit dropped from 9,677 feet to 8,363 feet (U.S. Geological Survey, 2000).

Kīlauea, 2018: Lava flows in the Lower East Rift Zone destroyed 700 homes, displaced more than 2,000 people, and covered 30 miles of road (National Park Service, n.d.-a).

Yellowstone: Its three caldera-forming eruptions were among the largest on Earth. The National Park Service says another eruption in the next few hundred years is not likely, and the most likely future activity would be slow lava flows (National Park Service, n.d.-d).
